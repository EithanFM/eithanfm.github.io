## Context
In June 2026 I co-authored a team policy report for GED-0315 (Global Crises) at Hult International Business School. The report assessed refining and processing as the midstream chokepoint in the U.S. AI minerals supply chain under a Strait of Hormuz disruption scenario. I authored the recommendations section and contributed to the conclusion.

## Question or thesis
If the Strait of Hormuz closed, how would energy, import, and logistics shocks hit U.S. refining of AI-critical inputs, and what policy and industry response would close the midstream capacity gap?

## Method
As a team we mapped the midstream segment from mining through refining to chips, hardware, and data centers, identified import dependence on Chinese refining as the primary vulnerability, traced transmission pathways from a Hormuz disruption, and outlined an early-warning dashboard. My section set short-term and long-term recommendations for government and industry: stockpile drawdowns of refined products, Defense Production Act Title III support for domestic plants, Minerals Security Partnership coordination with allies, FAST-41 permitting acceleration, multi-month refined inventories, supplier diversification, and advanced recycling from e-waste and end-of-life magnets.

## Result
We delivered a group report (dated June 16, 2026) and supporting decks. The core finding was that U.S. AI hardware depends on refining chokepoints the country does not control, so short-term shock response only works if it funds lasting midstream capacity at home and with allies. No report file is published here (team deliverable).
